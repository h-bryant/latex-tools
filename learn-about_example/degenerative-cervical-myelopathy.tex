\begin{note}[In brief]
\begin{itemize}
  \item Degenerative cervical myelopathy (DCM) is a slow \glslink{sci}{spinal cord injury}: age-related narrowing of the spinal canal in the neck compresses the cord, and it is the commonest cause of spinal cord dysfunction in adults \citep{badhiwala2020,nouri2015}.
  \item About a quarter of healthy adults, and a third of those aged 60 and over, have \glslink{cordcompression}{cord compression} on MRI. Most have no symptoms, but roughly 2\% of adults meet clinical criteria for DCM, about ten times the share recorded in hospital data \citep{smith2021,grodzinski2023}.
  \item The early picture is numb, clumsy hands and an unsteady walk. It is routinely mistaken for \glslink{carpaltunnel}{carpal tunnel syndrome} or for aging, and diagnosis takes about 2 years and 5 physician visits on average \citep{behrbalk2013}.
  \item Left alone, 20 to 62\% of patients with symptoms deteriorate within 3 to 6 years, and damage that has accumulated is largely permanent \citep{fehlings2017cpg}.
  \item Decompressive surgery is recommended for moderate and severe disease and for mild disease that worsens. It improves function by about 2 to 3 points on the 18-point \glslink{mjoa}{mJOA} scale on average, but few patients recover fully \citep{fehlings2017cpg,fehlings2015}.
  \item Operating from the front or the back of the neck gives similar functional results with different complications; overall complication rates are 14 to 19\%, and most complications are temporary \citep{ghogawala2021,fehlings2012}.
  \item No drug has yet been shown to protect the cord. The frontiers are earlier diagnosis, \glslink{qmri}{quantitative MRI}, and add-on \glslink{neuroprotection}{neuroprotective} drugs tested alongside surgery \citep{fehlings2021,badhiwala2020}.
\end{itemize}
\end{note}

\section{A slow spinal cord injury caused by a wearing neck}

Degenerative cervical myelopathy is what happens when the ordinary wear of the neck's spine, called \gls{spondylosis}, narrows the \glslink{spinalcanal}{spinal canal} enough to injure the spinal cord inside it. The name says where and why: \emph{cervical} is the neck, \emph{\gls{myelopathy}} is disease of the spinal cord itself, and \emph{degenerative} distinguishes it from cord injury by trauma, tumor, infection, or inflammation. An international consensus led by AO Spine and the charity Myelopathy.org settled in 2022 on a one-line definition, “a progressive spinal cord injury caused by narrowing of the cervical spinal canal,” after finding the disease had been going by more than 11 different names \citep{davies2024def}.

The umbrella term was proposed in 2015 to gather several conditions that had been studied separately \citep{nouri2015}. The largest is \glslink{csm}{cervical spondylotic myelopathy}, in which bulging discs, \glslink{osteophyte}{bony spurs}, and thickened ligaments do the compressing. A second is \glslink{opll}{ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament}, in which a ligament along the front wall of the canal turns to bone; it is far more common in East Asia. A third contributor is a canal that was narrow from birth, which leaves less room for any later degeneration \citep{nouri2015,bajwa2012}.

The condition matters for three reasons. It is the leading cause of spinal cord dysfunction in adults worldwide, and its frequency is rising as populations age \citep{badhiwala2020}. It is treatable: surgery reliably halts progression and usually brings some recovery, so the disability it causes is in large part avoidable \citep{fehlings2017cpg}. And it is badly under-recognized. Modeling against UK hospital records suggests that most cases, particularly in the elderly, are never diagnosed, and the patients who are diagnosed have typically waited more than 2 years \citep{grodzinski2023,behrbalk2013}.

\begin{note}[Two sentences to remember]
Degenerative cervical myelopathy is a slow spinal cord injury caused by age-related narrowing of the canal in the neck; it produces numb, clumsy hands, an unsteady gait, and bladder urgency, and it progresses in steps. Surgery to make room for the cord is the only treatment shown to stop the decline, so the whole game is recognizing it early.
\end{note}

\section{How a narrowed canal injures the cord}

The cervical spinal cord runs through a bony tunnel formed by seven stacked vertebrae. In front of the cord lie the vertebral bodies and the \glslink{intervertebraldisc}{intervertebral discs} between them; behind it lie the bony arches (laminae) lined by the elastic \gls{ligamentumflavum}; to each side sit the facet joints. In a healthy adult the cord floats in a cuff of cerebrospinal fluid with room to spare. Degeneration takes that room away from both directions at once (Figure 1).

\subsection*{Static compression: discs, spurs, ligaments, and a narrow canal to begin with}

The sequence usually starts in the disc. With age the disc loses water and height and bulges backward toward the canal. The vertebrae above and below respond by growing \glslink{osteophyte}{osteophytes}, bony spurs along the disc margins; together the bulging disc and its spurs form a disc-osteophyte complex that indents the front of the cord. Loss of disc height also slackens the ligamentum flavum behind the cord, which thickens and buckles inward, while the facet joints enlarge with arthritis. Reviews of the pathogenesis describe the result as static compression from the front and the back at the same level \citep{karadimas2013,nouri2015}.

Two variants change the arithmetic. In \glslink{opll}{OPLL}, the ligament that runs down the back of the vertebral bodies calcifies into a bar of bone that can span several levels; radiographic surveys in Japan find it in 1.9 to 4.3\% of adults over 30, and genetics play a strong part \citep{matsunaga2012}. Congenital \glslink{stenosis}{cervical stenosis} is the other. In a study of 1,066 adult skeletons, a canal narrower than 13 mm from front to back fell more than 2 standard deviations below the mean, and this is the usual threshold for calling a canal congenitally narrow \citep{bajwa2012}. A related radiographic index, the \glslink{torgpavlov}{Torg–Pavlov ratio}, flags the same thing. In the AO Spine surgical cohorts, about 8\% of patients had a cord that filled 70\% or more of the canal, and they presented 5 years younger than the rest \citep{nouri2022}.

\subsection*{Dynamic injury: the neck moves}

The cord is not compressed in one fixed position. When the neck flexes, the cord lengthens and is stretched over the spurs in front of it; when the neck extends, the ligamentum flavum buckles inward and the canal shortens and narrows. Each movement adds a pinch or a stretch to a cord that already has no slack, and thousands of repetitions a day amount to a chronic, repetitive injury. Reviews of the pathophysiology treat this dynamic component, together with the altered tension and blood supply that come from a poorly aligned spine, as central to why compression that looks modest on a still image can injure the cord \citep{karadimas2013,nouri2015}.

\subsection*{What happens inside the cord}

Compression injures the cord mainly by starving it. The small arteries that feed the cord's core are pinched, producing chronic \gls{ischemia}, and tissue from patients and animal models shows the molecular signature of oxygen shortage. The \glslink{bscb}{blood–spinal cord barrier} becomes leaky, letting inflammatory cells and proteins into the tissue, and activated immune cells release inflammatory signals. \glslink{oligodendrocyte}{Oligodendrocytes}, the cells that maintain the insulating myelin on nerve fibers, die by programmed cell death, and the long motor and sensory tracts in the \glslink{whitematter}{white matter} \glslink{demyelination}{lose their myelin} and then their axons. In the \glslink{graymatter}{gray matter}, the motor neurons of the front horns at the compressed level are lost \citep{akter2020,yu2011}. The cascade differs from that of an acute traumatic cord injury in both its nature and its time course, which is one reason drugs developed for trauma cannot simply be transplanted to DCM \citep{karadimas2013}.

This anatomy explains the symptoms. Damage to the \gls{corticospinaltract} below the compression produces weak, \glslink{spasticity}{stiff} legs with brisk reflexes. Damage to the \gls{dorsalcolumns} takes away fine touch and position sense, so the hands become clumsy and the gait unsteady, especially in the dark. Loss of gray matter at the level of compression wastes and weakens the hand muscles, and interruption of the pathways to the bladder produces urgency \citep{akter2020,tetreault2022}.

\begin{note}[Why the picture and the patient disagree]
The degree of compression on an \glslink{mri}{MRI} correlates poorly with how ill the patient is, and mild-looking compression can cause severe disease \citep{davies2018}. Canal size varies between people, the dynamic component is invisible on a still image, and the cord adapts to slow change. The corollary for diagnosis is that the scan must be read alongside the examination, not instead of it.
\end{note}

\subsection*{Why recovery after decompression is incomplete}

Decompression stops the injury but does not undo it. Animal work shows that suddenly restoring blood flow to a chronically starved cord produces its own \glslink{reperfusioninjury}{ischemia-reperfusion injury}, which the drug \gls{riluzole} blocked in rats, and the same report cites surgical cohort data in which 9.3\% of patients declined functionally after decompression and 44\% remained substantially impaired 6 months later \citep{karadimas2015}. Some plasticity does occur after decompression, with markers of axon growth rising, but lost neurons are not replaced, and delayed decompression in animals leaves more scarring and less blood flow. The upshot is a therapeutic window: the earlier the cord is freed, the more there is left to recover \citep{akter2020}.

Genetics contribute at every stage. A 2020 \glslink{systematicreview}{systematic review} found 28 candidate genes linked to whether degeneration develops, whether it injures the cord, how severe the injury is, and how the patient responds to surgery, but the studies were small and mostly unreplicated \citep{pope2020genetics}.

\section{Common, mostly silent, and mostly undiagnosed}

The \glslink{prevalence}{prevalence} of degenerative cervical myelopathy is uncertain, and the uncertainty is itself the main epidemiological fact (Figure 2). The best pooled estimate of \glslink{cordcompression}{cord compression} on \glslink{mri}{MRI} in people scanned for unrelated reasons is 24.2\% (95\% CI 12.4 to 36) across 11 studies and 3,686 people, rising from 7.4\% under age 60 to 35.3\% at 60 and over \citep{smith2021}. Among people whose scans were prompted by symptoms of myelopathy, 81\% had compression. The same review pooled three studies of healthy volunteers who were also examined clinically and found that 2.3\% (95\% CI 1.4 to 3.1) met criteria for DCM, which is the basis for the widely quoted figure of “1 in 50 adults” \citep{smith2021,davies2024cos}.

Administrative data tell a different story. Hospital-recorded prevalence in England was 0.19\% of adults (95\% CI 0.17 to 0.21), roughly a twelfth of the 2.2\% that the compression data imply, and the two estimates diverge most in the old: recorded prevalence peaks at 0.42\% at ages 50 to 54 and falls to 0.12\% after 79, while the compression-based estimate keeps rising to about 4\% in the same age group \citep{grodzinski2023}. Part of the gap is coding: DCM has had no specific \glslink{icd}{ICD code}, so cases scatter across several codes \citep{nouri2015}. Part is clinical: unsteadiness and clumsy hands in an 80-year-old are readily attributed to age.

Estimates based on treated cases are therefore minimums. A Dutch neurosurgical practice serving 1.7 million people operated on 1.6 per 100,000 inhabitants per year \citep{boogaarts2015}. A 12-year national cohort in Taiwan found hospitalization for the condition at 4.04 per 100,000 person-years, higher in men and rising steeply with age \citep{wu2013}. Combining nontraumatic \glslink{sci}{spinal cord injury} data, the 2015 review put the North American incidence at a minimum of 41 per million per year and the prevalence at a minimum of 605 per million \citep{nouri2015}.

\subsection*{Who gets it}

Patients in the large surgical cohorts were in their mid-50s on average, and about two thirds were men: the international AO Spine cohort of 479 patients had a mean age of 56.4 years and was 65\% male \citep{fehlings2015}. Regional differences are striking. Pooled compression prevalence in healthy American and European populations (39.7\%) was more than three times that in Asian populations (11.1\%), a difference the authors could not fully explain, while \glslink{opll}{OPLL} runs the other way, being far more common in East Asian than in Western populations \citep{smith2021,matsunaga2012,sakai2022}.

A final population is people who have already fallen. In a prospective study at a trauma center, 18\% of cognitively intact patients admitted with a hip fracture after a fall had clinical findings of previously undiagnosed cervical myelopathy, against none of the age-matched controls having elective hip replacement \citep{radcliff2016}.

\section{Why the first symptoms are so easy to dismiss}

Degenerative cervical myelopathy announces itself with symptoms that each have a more familiar explanation. A scoping review of 61 studies found that the commonest complaints were \glslink{paresthesia}{paresthesias} of some kind (86\% of patients), hand numbness (82\%), hand paresthesias (79\%), and impaired gait (72\%), with neck or shoulder pain in about half \citep{jiang2024symptoms}. \glslink{autonomic}{Bladder symptoms}, usually urgency or hesitancy, were reported by 38\% and bowel symptoms by 23\%. In a survey of 171 people living with the condition, poor balance was the single most common current symptom (84\%), yet the commonest \emph{first} symptom was neck pain, and 80\% had waited more than 6 months for a diagnosis; a feeling of heavy legs was the only initial symptom associated with being diagnosed early \citep{munro2023}.

The pattern is more telling than any single symptom. Symptoms are typically in both hands rather than one, involve the legs as well as the arms, spare the face, and progress over months to years, often in steps with plateaus between \citep{tetreault2022,milligan2019}. Patients describe dropping objects, deteriorating handwriting, difficulty with buttons and keys, needing the handrail on stairs, and urgency with little warning. A shock-like sensation shooting down the spine on bending the neck, the \glslink{lhermitte}{Lhermitte sign}, appears in more severe cases \citep{davies2018}.

\subsection*{What the examination shows, and what it misses}

The examination looks for the signature of cord injury: \glslink{myelopathicsigns}{upper motor neuron signs} below the level of compression, sometimes with \glslink{lmnsigns}{lower motor neuron signs} in the arms at that level. A \glslink{systematicreview}{systematic review} pooling case-control studies ranked the signs by how well they rule the diagnosis in or out \citep{jiang2024signs}. The most sensitive were \gls{hyperreflexia} and the \glslink{tromner}{Trömner sign}; the most specific were the \glslink{babinski}{Babinski sign}, \gls{clonus}, the Trömner sign, and the \glslink{invertedsupinator}{inverted supinator sign}. The familiar \glslink{hoffmann}{Hoffmann sign} performed inconsistently across studies.

\begin{flag}[A normal examination does not exclude the diagnosis]
In a prospective surgical series, 21\% of patients who had cervical myelopathy confirmed at surgery, and who improved afterward, had no myelopathic signs at all on examination \citep{rhee2009}. Examination findings have low sensitivity and high specificity: when present they are strong evidence, when absent they prove little \citep{davies2018}. Persistent bilateral hand numbness with any gait change deserves an \glslink{mri}{MRI} regardless of the reflexes.
\end{flag}

\subsection*{Grading severity}

Two scales organize everything else in this briefing. The \glslink{mjoa}{modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association score}, adapted by \citet{benzel1991} from a Japanese scale to replace chopsticks with a spoon, rates four domains for a maximum of 18 points \citep{margetis2025}. It is the primary outcome in nearly every treatment study, and analysis of 757 patients in the AO Spine cohorts defined mild disease as 15 to 17, moderate as 12 to 14, and severe as 11 or less \citep{tetreault2017thresholds}. Its limitations matter for reading the literature: its four items hang together only moderately, with bladder function measuring something different from the rest; it has a ceiling that hides improvement in mild disease; and a one-point change can mean anything from slightly easier buttons to losing the ability to walk \citep{kopjar2015,tetreault2015mcid}. The \glslink{mcid}{minimum clinically important difference} is about 1 point for mild, 2 for moderate, and 3 for severe disease \citep{tetreault2015mcid}.

The older \glslink{nurick}{Nurick grade} rates walking alone, from 0 (root symptoms only) through 1 (cord signs with a normal gait), 2 (gait difficulty but working), 3 (gait difficulty preventing work but walking unaided), and 4 (walks only with help) to 5 (wheelchair or bed bound) \citep{nurick1972,margetis2025}. It is coarse, but its anchoring in employment and independence is what gave the mJOA severity bands their meaning.

\section{What happens without treatment}

The \glslink{naturalhistory}{natural history} of degenerative cervical myelopathy is known only in outline, because most patients diagnosed in the modern era have been operated on. Three questions have usable answers: how often silent compression becomes myelopathy, how often established myelopathy worsens, and how much a compressed cord raises the risk of sudden injury.

\subsection*{From silent compression to myelopathy}

The largest prospective study followed 199 people with \glslink{mri}{MRI} \glslink{cordcompression}{cord compression} but no myelopathy for 2 to 12 years: 8\% had developed clinical myelopathy by 12 months and 22.6\% by a median of 44 months \citep{bednarik2008}. These two figures, roughly 8\% at 1 year and 23\% at 4 years, anchor every guideline \citep{wilson2013,fehlings2017cpg}. The guideline group pooled the predictors of conversion as \glslink{relativerisk}{relative risks} (table below). A later cohort of 112 patients found 13.4\% converting over a median of 3 years, with \gls{radiculopathy}, a cord cross-sectional area of 70 mm² or less, and a \gls{compressionratio} of 0.4 or less as the independent predictors \citep{kadanka2017}.

\subsection*{Once myelopathy is established}

\glslink{systematicreview}{Systematic reviews} of patients managed without surgery find that 20 to 62\% deteriorate by at least one point on the Japanese Orthopaedic Association scale over 3 to 6 years \citep{fehlings2017cpg,karadimas2013}. The spread reflects who was studied: the only randomized trial, which enrolled 64 Czech patients with mild or moderate, slowly progressive disease and followed them for 10 years, found that median \glslink{mjoa}{mJOA} scores were unchanged in both the conservative and the surgical arm, with about half of each group somewhat worse by the end and no difference between them \citep{kadanka2011}. Cohorts including more severe or faster-moving disease show higher deterioration rates. Progression is typically stepwise, with long plateaus punctuated by declines \citep{milligan2019}, and the end state of unchecked progression is \gls{tetraplegia} and wheelchair dependence \citep{davies2018}.

\subsection*{A compressed cord is a vulnerable cord}

A cord with no reserve is more easily injured by a fall or a whiplash. In the Taiwanese national cohort, patients with \glslink{csm}{cervical spondylotic myelopathy} managed without surgery were hospitalized for \glslink{sci}{spinal cord injury} at 13.9 per 1,000 person-years, against 9.4 among those who had surgery, an adjusted \glslink{hazardratio}{hazard ratio} of 1.57 \citep{wu2013}; both figures are far above the rate in the general population of about 0.18 per 1,000 \citep{fehlings2017cpg}. A later analysis of the same database, covering 17,258 patients, put the overall rate at 2.0 per 1,000 person-years, highest in unoperated patients with \glslink{opll}{OPLL} \citep{chen2016}. The hip-fracture study described above closes the loop: undiagnosed myelopathy causes falls, and falls injure the cord further \citep{radcliff2016}.

\begin{note}[What the natural history data can and cannot say]
Every number above comes from selected patients: those referred to a center, those mild enough to be left unoperated, or those who happened to have an MRI. None is a population estimate. The guideline group rated the evidence for deterioration rates as moderate and for the hospitalization rates as low \citep{fehlings2017cpg}.
\end{note}

\section{Getting to an MRI is the bottleneck}

Diagnosis requires only two things: a clinician who thinks of the condition and a cervical \glslink{mri}{MRI} that shows \glslink{cordcompression}{cord compression} matching the clinical picture. The delay lives almost entirely in the first step. In a series of 42 patients who eventually had surgery, the mean time from first symptom to diagnosis was 2.2 years (SD 2.3), after a mean of 5.2 physician visits; 69\% had first consulted a family physician, 43\% had been diagnosed initially with \glslink{carpaltunnel}{carpal tunnel syndrome}, and family physicians made the correct diagnosis in under 5\% of cases \citep{behrbalk2013}.

An online survey of 778 patients found that a delay of 1 to 2 years was typical, that longer delays were associated with greater disability, dependence on others, and unemployment, and that Black or African American respondents faced longer delays \citep{pope2020delays}.

\begin{flag}[Who should be suspected]
Anyone over 50 with progressive numbness or clumsiness in both hands, unsteadiness or falls, stiff legs, or new urinary urgency, especially in combination, needs a full neurological examination and, if there is any sign of cord involvement, a cervical MRI \citep{davies2018}. Hand symptoms in both hands at once, or hand symptoms with any leg or bladder symptom, are not carpal tunnel syndrome until proven otherwise. Rapid onset, fever, weight loss, or recent surgery point to other causes and need urgent assessment \citep{tetreault2022}.
\end{flag}

\subsection*{Reading the MRI}

MRI shows the discs, ligaments, and cord directly and is the essential test; X-rays and CT show bone but not the cord \citep{davies2018}. The radiologist reports where and how much the cord is compressed, how many levels are involved, and whether the cord's own signal has changed. A bright spot on T2-weighted images, \glslink{t2hyperintensity}{T2 hyperintensity}, indicates excess water from swelling or tissue loss; it is common but nonspecific and may or may not be reversible. A dark area on T1-weighted images, \glslink{t1hypointensity}{T1 hypointensity}, indicates established tissue loss, or \gls{myelomalacia} \citep{tetreault2022}. A \glslink{systematicreview}{systematic review} of prognostic imaging found that the number of T2-bright segments, combined T1 and T2 change, and the ratio of signal intensities predicted a poorer surgical outcome, whereas the T2 grade alone, the \glslink{compressionratio}{compression ratio}, and the canal diameter did not \citep{tetreault2013mri}. Because a quarter of healthy adults have compression, a compressed cord on MRI confirms the diagnosis only when it fits the symptoms and signs \citep{tetreault2022}.

\subsection*{Electrophysiology and other tests}

Electrical tests are not needed for diagnosis but earn their place in three situations \citep{tetreault2022}. \glslink{emg}{Electromyography} and nerve conduction studies distinguish cord disease from carpal tunnel syndrome, peripheral neuropathy, and motor neuron disease; nerve conduction is typically normal in DCM. \glslink{ssep}{Somatosensory evoked potentials} and \glslink{mep}{motor evoked potentials} measure conduction through the cord itself and help when the scan and the examination disagree, or when someone with silent compression is being watched for progression. Blood tests for vitamin B12, copper, and inflammatory markers are routine because they exclude treatable mimics.

The absence of agreed diagnostic criteria compounds the problem. There is no validated definition of what combination of symptoms, signs, and imaging constitutes DCM, and developing one is a stated research priority \citep{hilton2022}.

\section{Surgery is the only treatment shown to stop the decline}

Treatment decisions follow severity (Figure 4). The 2017 guideline from AO Spine and the Cervical Spine Research Society, built on five \glslink{systematicreview}{systematic reviews} and graded with the \glslink{grade}{GRADE} system, makes four recommendations \citep{fehlings2017cpg}. For severe and moderate disease (\glslink{mjoa}{mJOA} 14 or below) it recommends surgery, a strong recommendation on moderate-quality evidence. For mild disease (15 to 17) it suggests offering either surgery or a supervised trial of \glslink{nonop}{structured rehabilitation}, recommends surgery if the patient deteriorates, and suggests it if they fail to improve; this is a weak recommendation on very low to low-quality evidence. For people with \glslink{cordcompression}{cord compression} but no myelopathy it suggests against prophylactic surgery, with counseling about the risk and education about warning symptoms, unless there is also \gls{radiculopathy}, in which case surgery or close follow-up are both options because the risk of progression is about threefold.

\subsection*{The mild-disease question}

Mild disease is where the evidence is thinnest and practice most varied. The one randomized trial found no average difference between surgery and conservative care at 3 and at 10 years in 64 patients with mild or moderate, slowly progressive disease \citep{kadanka2011}. Against that, 193 patients with mild disease in the AO Spine cohorts had quality-of-life scores more than two standard deviations below population norms before surgery and improved significantly on every measure 2 years afterward, though the mJOA gain (0.87 points) was below the \glslink{mcid}{minimum clinically important difference} while disability and quality-of-life gains were substantial \citep{badhiwala2019}. A 2025 AO Spine appraisal of recent literature issued a conditional recommendation that surgery is a valid option for mild disease \citep{fehlings2025}. Meanwhile a survey of 699 spine professionals worldwide found that most offer nonoperative patients standardized follow-up, most often at 6-month intervals, but that what the follow-up consists of, and what would tip them toward surgery, varied widely \citep{brannigan2024}.

\subsection*{What nonoperative care can and cannot do}

No nonoperative treatment has been shown to reverse cord injury or to change the \glslink{naturalhistory}{natural history} \citep{rhee2013}. Structured rehabilitation for mild disease means supervised physiotherapy for strength and balance, activity advice, sometimes a soft collar, and scheduled reassessment to catch deterioration. Two things are to be avoided: cervical traction and spinal manipulation, which can injure a compressed cord and are considered contraindicated \citep{davies2018,mccormick2020}. Pain and \gls{spasticity} are treated on their own merits with neuropathic analgesics and antispasticity drugs, before and after surgery \citep{davies2018}.

\subsection*{Operating from the front or the back}

Surgery makes room for the cord. From the front of the neck, \glslink{acdf}{anterior cervical discectomy and fusion} removes the offending disc and \glslink{osteophyte}{spurs} at one or two levels and \glslink{fusion}{fuses} the vertebrae; a \gls{corpectomy} removes a vertebral body when compression extends behind it. From the back, \gls{laminectomy} removes the bony roof of the canal, usually with screws and rods to prevent the neck from later slumping, while \gls{laminoplasty} hinges the roof open and props it, preserving motion. The choice turns on where the compression comes from, how many levels are involved, and the alignment of the neck: anterior approaches suit focal compression from the front, OPLL, and a \glslink{kyphosis}{kyphotic} neck; posterior approaches suit compression at three or more levels or from behind \citep{mccormick2020,badhiwala2020}.

The only randomized comparison, 163 patients with two or more levels of compression, found no difference in patient-reported physical function at 1 year between ventral and dorsal surgery (gains of 5.9 versus 6.2 points on the SF-36 physical score), but complications were twice as frequent after ventral surgery (48\% versus 24\%), almost entirely temporary \gls{dysphagia} (41\% versus none), while dorsal surgery carried more new neurological deficits (9\% versus 2\%) and readmissions \citep{ghogawala2021}. In a nonrandomized comparison within the same trial, laminoplasty had the fewest complications (10.7\%). The 2025 AO Spine appraisal concluded that both approaches afford similar benefit with different complication profiles \citep{fehlings2025}.

\subsection*{What surgery achieves}

In the international AO Spine cohort of 479 patients, mean mJOA improved from 12.5 before surgery to 14.9 at 2 years, neck-related disability fell by a third, and physical and mental quality-of-life scores rose by about 6.5 points each; neurological complications occurred in 3.1\% \citep{fehlings2015}. The gain scales with severity, as the pooled analysis below shows: the sickest patients gain the most points but also start furthest from normal. Timing matters. In the AO Spine cohorts, patients operated on within 4 months of symptom onset had significantly better mJOA outcomes than those operated on later, although the mean duration of symptoms before surgery was 26 months \citep{tetreault2019}. Most recovery occurs within 6 to 12 months of surgery, and deficits that remain after that are likely permanent \citep{davies2018}.

Overall complication rates in the North American cohort were 11\% after anterior-only and 19\% after posterior-only surgery, and older age, longer operations, and combined front-and-back procedures raised the risk \citep{fehlings2012}. The guideline's cost-effectiveness review, based on a Canadian cohort followed for 2 years and modeled over a lifetime, found surgery cost about CAD 11,500 per \glslink{qaly}{quality-adjusted life year} gained, or about CAD 20,500 under the conservative assumption that unoperated patients would never decline; both are well within conventional willingness-to-pay thresholds \citep{witiw2017,fehlings2017cpg}.

\section{Recovery is real but partial, and time-limited}

Most patients improve after surgery and nearly all stop declining, but few return to normal. Alongside the average gains above, surgical cohort data cited by \citet{karadimas2015} put the share of patients still substantially impaired 6 months after decompression at 44\%, with 9.3\% having declined. The research community's own summary is that surgery offers most patients meaningful benefit, that few make a full recovery, and that the condition carries among the worst quality-of-life scores of any chronic disease, with high levels of dependence and unemployment \citep{davies2022why}. Residual problems commonly include falls, reduced mobility, incontinence, pain, depression, and poor sleep \citep{davies2018}.

The predictors of a poorer outcome are the ones this briefing has already met: more severe disease at presentation, longer duration of symptoms, and \glslink{mri}{MRI} evidence of established tissue loss, particularly T1 change combined with T2 change \citep{tetreault2019,tetreault2013mri}. Because these are largely consequences of delay, prognosis is to a large extent a function of how quickly the diagnosis was made. The survey evidence that longer delays go with greater disability, dependence, and unemployment makes the same point from the patient's side \citep{pope2020delays}.

Living with the condition after surgery means managing what remains. Neuropathic pain and \gls{spasticity} are treated with medication, balance and hand function with rehabilitation, and the neck itself with sensible activity; the value of \glslink{nonop}{structured rehabilitation} after surgery is one of the ten research priorities precisely because it has been so little studied \citep{davies2022why}. The patient charity Myelopathy.org, a registered charity in England and Wales, runs the largest international community for people with DCM and their carers and co-leads the research program described below.

\section{Open questions, live controversies, and what is coming}

The field organized its uncertainties in 2019 through \glslink{recodedcm}{AO Spine RECODE-DCM}, a partnership of surgeons, other clinicians, and people living with the condition from 68 countries, which used a \glslink{jla}{James Lind Alliance priority setting partnership} to rank the ten research questions that matter most \citep{davies2022why}. The list is a fair map of where the evidence is missing: awareness, \glslink{naturalhistory}{natural history}, diagnostic criteria, assessment tools, the biological basis of injury, perioperative rehabilitation, novel therapies, socioeconomic impact, dynamic imaging and neurophysiology, and individualizing surgery. The same initiative produced the unifying name and definition and, in 2024, a core outcome set and minimum data set for future trials \citep{davies2024def,davies2024cos}.

\textbf{Whom to operate on, and when.} The mild-disease question described above is the central controversy in practice. The 2017 guideline's weak recommendation, the null randomized trial, the favorable cohort data, the 2025 conditional recommendation for surgery, and the wide variation in what surgeons actually do are all consistent with genuine equipoise \citep{fehlings2017cpg,kadanka2011,badhiwala2019,fehlings2025,brannigan2024}. Trials that randomize mild disease with better outcome measures are the obvious next step, and identifying which mild patients are on a downward trajectory is a stated priority.

\textbf{Measuring what matters.} The \glslink{mjoa}{mJOA} is the field's common currency and a poor one: its ceiling hides change in mild disease, its steps are not equal, and it ignores pain, which patients rank highly \citep{tetreault2015mcid,kopjar2015}. The \gls{riluzole} trial's authors concluded that the scale was too insensitive to detect a drug effect \citep{fehlings2021}. The 2024 core outcome set is the field's answer, and a 2025 AO Spine review argues for multidimensional assessment including pain, quality of life, frailty, and imaging \citep{davies2024cos,fehlings2025}.

\textbf{Neuroprotection.} Riluzole, which protected the cord in animal models, was tested in 290 patients undergoing decompression in the CSM-Protect trial: the change in mJOA at 6 months was 2.45 points with riluzole and 2.83 with placebo (difference \ensuremath{-}0.38, 95\% CI \ensuremath{-}0.90 to 0.13), a null result on the primary outcome, with a signal of less neck pain in secondary analyses \citep{fehlings2021}. The anti-inflammatory drug \gls{ibudilast} is being tested as an add-on to surgery in the UK RECEDE-Myelopathy trial, with pain and mJOA at 6 months as co-primary endpoints \citep{davies2023recede}; when this briefing was compiled the trial registry still listed it as recruiting and no results had been published.

\textbf{Seeing injury before it is felt.} \glslink{qmri}{Quantitative MRI} measures cord area, \glslink{dti}{diffusion} along fibers, and myelin content directly. In a longitudinal study of 26 nonoperative patients, such measures flagged progression in 68\% over about 13 months, against 11.5\% by mJOA and 42\% by the patients' own impression, suggesting that behavioral adaptation masks ongoing tissue injury \citep{martin2018}. The 2025 AO Spine appraisal conditionally recommends diffusion-weighted signal change for judging prognosis after surgery \citep{fehlings2025}. Whether these tools can decide who with silent compression should be operated on is the practical question.

\textbf{Counting the undiagnosed.} The twelvefold gap between modeled and recorded \glslink{prevalence}{prevalence}, concentrated in the elderly, implies a large population living with a treatable cause of falls, incontinence, and dependence that is being attributed to age \citep{grodzinski2023}. Diagnostic criteria, screening tools for primary care, and a dedicated diagnostic code are the prerequisites for closing it \citep{hilton2022,nouri2015}, and the evidence that delays fall unequally on Black patients makes this a matter of equity as well as efficiency \citep{pope2020delays}.
