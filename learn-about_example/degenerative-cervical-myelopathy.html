Degenerative cervical myelopathy

How age-related narrowing of the neck's spinal canal slowly injures the spinal cord, who it affects, how it is recognized and treated, and where the evidence is thin. For a scientifically literate reader new to the field.1

Overview

A slow spinal cord injury caused by a wearing neck

In brief
  • Degenerative cervical myelopathy (DCM) is a slow spinal cord injury: age-related narrowing of the spinal canal in the neck compresses the cord, and it is the commonest cause of spinal cord dysfunction in adults (Badhiwala et al., 2020; Nouri et al., 2015).
  • About a quarter of healthy adults, and a third of those aged 60 and over, have cord compression on MRI. Most have no symptoms, but roughly 2% of adults meet clinical criteria for DCM, about ten times the share recorded in hospital data (Smith et al., 2021; Grodzinski et al., 2023).
  • The early picture is numb, clumsy hands and an unsteady walk. It is routinely mistaken for carpal tunnel syndrome or for aging, and diagnosis takes about 2 years and 5 physician visits on average (Behrbalk et al., 2013).
  • Left alone, 20 to 62% of patients with symptoms deteriorate within 3 to 6 years, and damage that has accumulated is largely permanent (Fehlings et al., 2017b).
  • Decompressive surgery is recommended for moderate and severe disease and for mild disease that worsens. It improves function by about 2 to 3 points on the 18-point mJOA scale on average, but few patients recover fully (Fehlings et al., 2017b; Fehlings et al., 2015).
  • Operating from the front or the back of the neck gives similar functional results with different complications; overall complication rates are 14 to 19%, and most complications are temporary (Ghogawala et al., 2021; Fehlings et al., 2012).
  • No drug has yet been shown to protect the cord. The frontiers are earlier diagnosis, quantitative MRI, and add-on neuroprotective drugs tested alongside surgery (Fehlings et al., 2021; Badhiwala et al., 2020).

Degenerative cervical myelopathy is what happens when the ordinary wear of the neck's spine, called spondylosis, narrows the spinal canal enough to injure the spinal cord inside it. The name says where and why: cervical is the neck, myelopathy is disease of the spinal cord itself, and degenerative distinguishes it from cord injury by trauma, tumor, infection, or inflammation. An international consensus led by AO Spine and the charity Myelopathy.org settled in 2022 on a one-line definition, “a progressive spinal cord injury caused by narrowing of the cervical spinal canal,” after finding the disease had been going by more than 11 different names (Davies et al., 2024a).

The umbrella term was proposed in 2015 to gather several conditions that had been studied separately (Nouri et al., 2015). The largest is cervical spondylotic myelopathy, in which bulging discs, bony spurs, and thickened ligaments do the compressing. A second is ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament, in which a ligament along the front wall of the canal turns to bone; it is far more common in East Asia. A third contributor is a canal that was narrow from birth, which leaves less room for any later degeneration (Nouri et al., 2015; Bajwa et al., 2012).

The condition matters for three reasons. It is the leading cause of spinal cord dysfunction in adults worldwide, and its frequency is rising as populations age (Badhiwala et al., 2020). It is treatable: surgery reliably halts progression and usually brings some recovery, so the disability it causes is in large part avoidable (Fehlings et al., 2017b). And it is badly under-recognized. Modeling against UK hospital records suggests that most cases, particularly in the elderly, are never diagnosed, and the patients who are diagnosed have typically waited more than 2 years (Grodzinski et al., 2023; Behrbalk et al., 2013).

Two sentences to remember

Degenerative cervical myelopathy is a slow spinal cord injury caused by age-related narrowing of the canal in the neck; it produces numb, clumsy hands, an unsteady gait, and bladder urgency, and it progresses in steps. Surgery to make room for the cord is the only treatment shown to stop the decline, so the whole game is recognizing it early.

Mechanism

How a narrowed canal injures the cord

The cervical spinal cord runs through a bony tunnel formed by seven stacked vertebrae. In front of the cord lie the vertebral bodies and the intervertebral discs between them; behind it lie the bony arches (laminae) lined by the elastic ligamentum flavum; to each side sit the facet joints. In a healthy adult the cord floats in a cuff of cerebrospinal fluid with room to spare. Degeneration takes that room away from both directions at once (Figure 1).

Static compression: discs, spurs, ligaments, and a narrow canal to begin with

The sequence usually starts in the disc. With age the disc loses water and height and bulges backward toward the canal. The vertebrae above and below respond by growing osteophytes, bony spurs along the disc margins; together the bulging disc and its spurs form a disc-osteophyte complex that indents the front of the cord. Loss of disc height also slackens the ligamentum flavum behind the cord, which thickens and buckles inward, while the facet joints enlarge with arthritis. Reviews of the pathogenesis describe the result as static compression from the front and the back at the same level (Karadimas et al., 2013; Nouri et al., 2015).

Two variants change the arithmetic. In OPLL, the ligament that runs down the back of the vertebral bodies calcifies into a bar of bone that can span several levels; radiographic surveys in Japan find it in 1.9 to 4.3% of adults over 30, and genetics play a strong part (Matsunaga and Sakou, 2012). Congenital cervical stenosis is the other. In a study of 1,066 adult skeletons, a canal narrower than 13 mm from front to back fell more than 2 standard deviations below the mean, and this is the usual threshold for calling a canal congenitally narrow (Bajwa et al., 2012). A related radiographic index, the Torg–Pavlov ratio, flags the same thing. In the AO Spine surgical cohorts, about 8% of patients had a cord that filled 70% or more of the canal, and they presented 5 years younger than the rest (Nouri et al., 2022).

Dynamic injury: the neck moves

The cord is not compressed in one fixed position. When the neck flexes, the cord lengthens and is stretched over the spurs in front of it; when the neck extends, the ligamentum flavum buckles inward and the canal shortens and narrows. Each movement adds a pinch or a stretch to a cord that already has no slack, and thousands of repetitions a day amount to a chronic, repetitive injury. Reviews of the pathophysiology treat this dynamic component, together with the altered tension and blood supply that come from a poorly aligned spine, as central to why compression that looks modest on a still image can injure the cord (Karadimas et al., 2013; Nouri et al., 2015).

What happens inside the cord

Compression injures the cord mainly by starving it. The small arteries that feed the cord's core are pinched, producing chronic ischemia, and tissue from patients and animal models shows the molecular signature of oxygen shortage. The blood–spinal cord barrier becomes leaky, letting inflammatory cells and proteins into the tissue, and activated immune cells release inflammatory signals. Oligodendrocytes, the cells that maintain the insulating myelin on nerve fibers, die by programmed cell death, and the long motor and sensory tracts in the white matter lose their myelin and then their axons. In the gray matter, the motor neurons of the front horns at the compressed level are lost (Akter et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2011). The cascade differs from that of an acute traumatic cord injury in both its nature and its time course, which is one reason drugs developed for trauma cannot simply be transplanted to DCM (Karadimas et al., 2013).

This anatomy explains the symptoms. Damage to the corticospinal tract below the compression produces weak, stiff legs with brisk reflexes. Damage to the dorsal columns takes away fine touch and position sense, so the hands become clumsy and the gait unsteady, especially in the dark. Loss of gray matter at the level of compression wastes and weakens the hand muscles, and interruption of the pathways to the bladder produces urgency (Akter et al., 2020; Tetreault et al., 2022).

Why the picture and the patient disagree

The degree of compression on an MRI correlates poorly with how ill the patient is, and mild-looking compression can cause severe disease (Davies et al., 2018). Canal size varies between people, the dynamic component is invisible on a still image, and the cord adapts to slow change. The corollary for diagnosis is that the scan must be read alongside the examination, not instead of it.

Why recovery after decompression is incomplete

Decompression stops the injury but does not undo it. Animal work shows that suddenly restoring blood flow to a chronically starved cord produces its own ischemia-reperfusion injury, which the drug riluzole blocked in rats, and the same report cites surgical cohort data in which 9.3% of patients declined functionally after decompression and 44% remained substantially impaired 6 months later (Karadimas et al., 2015). Some plasticity does occur after decompression, with markers of axon growth rising, but lost neurons are not replaced, and delayed decompression in animals leaves more scarring and less blood flow. The upshot is a therapeutic window: the earlier the cord is freed, the more there is left to recover (Akter et al., 2020).

Genetics contribute at every stage. A 2020 systematic review found 28 candidate genes linked to whether degeneration develops, whether it injures the cord, how severe the injury is, and how the patient responds to surgery, but the studies were small and mostly unreplicated (Pope et al., 2020a).

Who is affected

Common, mostly silent, and mostly undiagnosed

The prevalence of degenerative cervical myelopathy is uncertain, and the uncertainty is itself the main epidemiological fact (Figure 2). The best pooled estimate of cord compression on MRI in people scanned for unrelated reasons is 24.2% (95% CI 12.4 to 36) across 11 studies and 3,686 people, rising from 7.4% under age 60 to 35.3% at 60 and over (Smith et al., 2021). Among people whose scans were prompted by symptoms of myelopathy, 81% had compression. The same review pooled three studies of healthy volunteers who were also examined clinically and found that 2.3% (95% CI 1.4 to 3.1) met criteria for DCM, which is the basis for the widely quoted figure of “1 in 50 adults” (Smith et al., 2021; Davies et al., 2024b).

Administrative data tell a different story. Hospital-recorded prevalence in England was 0.19% of adults (95% CI 0.17 to 0.21), roughly a twelfth of the 2.2% that the compression data imply, and the two estimates diverge most in the old: recorded prevalence peaks at 0.42% at ages 50 to 54 and falls to 0.12% after 79, while the compression-based estimate keeps rising to about 4% in the same age group (Grodzinski et al., 2023). Part of the gap is coding: DCM has had no specific ICD code, so cases scatter across several codes (Nouri et al., 2015). Part is clinical: unsteadiness and clumsy hands in an 80-year-old are readily attributed to age.

Estimates based on treated cases are therefore minimums. A Dutch neurosurgical practice serving 1.7 million people operated on 1.6 per 100,000 inhabitants per year (Boogaarts and Bartels, 2015). A 12-year national cohort in Taiwan found hospitalization for the condition at 4.04 per 100,000 person-years, higher in men and rising steeply with age (Wu et al., 2013). Combining nontraumatic spinal cord injury data, the 2015 review put the North American incidence at a minimum of 41 per million per year and the prevalence at a minimum of 605 per million (Nouri et al., 2015).

Who gets it

Patients in the large surgical cohorts were in their mid-50s on average, and about two thirds were men: the international AO Spine cohort of 479 patients had a mean age of 56.4 years and was 65% male (Fehlings et al., 2015). Regional differences are striking. Pooled compression prevalence in healthy American and European populations (39.7%) was more than three times that in Asian populations (11.1%), a difference the authors could not fully explain, while OPLL runs the other way, being far more common in East Asian than in Western populations (Smith et al., 2021; Matsunaga and Sakou, 2012; Sakai et al., 2022).

A final population is people who have already fallen. In a prospective study at a trauma center, 18% of cognitively intact patients admitted with a hip fracture after a fall had clinical findings of previously undiagnosed cervical myelopathy, against none of the age-matched controls having elective hip replacement (Radcliff et al., 2016).

Symptoms and signs

Why the first symptoms are so easy to dismiss

Degenerative cervical myelopathy announces itself with symptoms that each have a more familiar explanation. A scoping review of 61 studies found that the commonest complaints were paresthesias of some kind (86% of patients), hand numbness (82%), hand paresthesias (79%), and impaired gait (72%), with neck or shoulder pain in about half (Jiang et al., 2024b). Bladder symptoms, usually urgency or hesitancy, were reported by 38% and bowel symptoms by 23%. In a survey of 171 people living with the condition, poor balance was the single most common current symptom (84%), yet the commonest first symptom was neck pain, and 80% had waited more than 6 months for a diagnosis; a feeling of heavy legs was the only initial symptom associated with being diagnosed early (Munro et al., 2023).

The pattern is more telling than any single symptom. Symptoms are typically in both hands rather than one, involve the legs as well as the arms, spare the face, and progress over months to years, often in steps with plateaus between (Tetreault et al., 2022; Milligan et al., 2019). Patients describe dropping objects, deteriorating handwriting, difficulty with buttons and keys, needing the handrail on stairs, and urgency with little warning. A shock-like sensation shooting down the spine on bending the neck, the Lhermitte sign, appears in more severe cases (Davies et al., 2018).

What the examination shows, and what it misses

The examination looks for the signature of cord injury: upper motor neuron signs below the level of compression, sometimes with lower motor neuron signs in the arms at that level. A systematic review pooling case-control studies ranked the signs by how well they rule the diagnosis in or out (Jiang et al., 2024a). The most sensitive were hyperreflexia and the Trömner sign; the most specific were the Babinski sign, clonus, the Trömner sign, and the inverted supinator sign. The familiar Hoffmann sign performed inconsistently across studies.

A normal examination does not exclude the diagnosis

In a prospective surgical series, 21% of patients who had cervical myelopathy confirmed at surgery, and who improved afterward, had no myelopathic signs at all on examination (Rhee et al., 2009). Examination findings have low sensitivity and high specificity: when present they are strong evidence, when absent they prove little (Davies et al., 2018). Persistent bilateral hand numbness with any gait change deserves an MRI regardless of the reflexes.

Grading severity

Two scales organize everything else in this briefing. The modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association score, adapted by Benzel et al. (1991) from a Japanese scale to replace chopsticks with a spoon, rates four domains for a maximum of 18 points (Margetis and Donnally, 2025). It is the primary outcome in nearly every treatment study, and analysis of 757 patients in the AO Spine cohorts defined mild disease as 15 to 17, moderate as 12 to 14, and severe as 11 or less (Tetreault et al., 2017). Its limitations matter for reading the literature: its four items hang together only moderately, with bladder function measuring something different from the rest; it has a ceiling that hides improvement in mild disease; and a one-point change can mean anything from slightly easier buttons to losing the ability to walk (Kopjar et al., 2015; Tetreault et al., 2015). The minimum clinically important difference is about 1 point for mild, 2 for moderate, and 3 for severe disease (Tetreault et al., 2015).

The older Nurick grade rates walking alone, from 0 (root symptoms only) through 1 (cord signs with a normal gait), 2 (gait difficulty but working), 3 (gait difficulty preventing work but walking unaided), and 4 (walks only with help) to 5 (wheelchair or bed bound) (Nurick, 1972; Margetis and Donnally, 2025). It is coarse, but its anchoring in employment and independence is what gave the mJOA severity bands their meaning.

Natural history

What happens without treatment

The natural history of degenerative cervical myelopathy is known only in outline, because most patients diagnosed in the modern era have been operated on. Three questions have usable answers: how often silent compression becomes myelopathy, how often established myelopathy worsens, and how much a compressed cord raises the risk of sudden injury.

From silent compression to myelopathy

The largest prospective study followed 199 people with MRI cord compression but no myelopathy for 2 to 12 years: 8% had developed clinical myelopathy by 12 months and 22.6% by a median of 44 months (Bednarik et al., 2008). These two figures, roughly 8% at 1 year and 23% at 4 years, anchor every guideline (Wilson et al., 2013; Fehlings et al., 2017b). The guideline group pooled the predictors of conversion as relative risks (table below). A later cohort of 112 patients found 13.4% converting over a median of 3 years, with radiculopathy, a cord cross-sectional area of 70 mm² or less, and a compression ratio of 0.4 or less as the independent predictors (Kadanka et al., 2017).

Once myelopathy is established

Systematic reviews of patients managed without surgery find that 20 to 62% deteriorate by at least one point on the Japanese Orthopaedic Association scale over 3 to 6 years (Fehlings et al., 2017b; Karadimas et al., 2013). The spread reflects who was studied: the only randomized trial, which enrolled 64 Czech patients with mild or moderate, slowly progressive disease and followed them for 10 years, found that median mJOA scores were unchanged in both the conservative and the surgical arm, with about half of each group somewhat worse by the end and no difference between them (Kadaňka et al., 2011). Cohorts including more severe or faster-moving disease show higher deterioration rates. Progression is typically stepwise, with long plateaus punctuated by declines (Milligan et al., 2019), and the end state of unchecked progression is tetraplegia and wheelchair dependence (Davies et al., 2018).

A compressed cord is a vulnerable cord

A cord with no reserve is more easily injured by a fall or a whiplash. In the Taiwanese national cohort, patients with cervical spondylotic myelopathy managed without surgery were hospitalized for spinal cord injury at 13.9 per 1,000 person-years, against 9.4 among those who had surgery, an adjusted hazard ratio of 1.57 (Wu et al., 2013); both figures are far above the rate in the general population of about 0.18 per 1,000 (Fehlings et al., 2017b). A later analysis of the same database, covering 17,258 patients, put the overall rate at 2.0 per 1,000 person-years, highest in unoperated patients with OPLL (Chen et al., 2016). The hip-fracture study described above closes the loop: undiagnosed myelopathy causes falls, and falls injure the cord further (Radcliff et al., 2016).

What the natural history data can and cannot say

Every number above comes from selected patients: those referred to a center, those mild enough to be left unoperated, or those who happened to have an MRI. None is a population estimate. The guideline group rated the evidence for deterioration rates as moderate and for the hospitalization rates as low (Fehlings et al., 2017b).

Diagnosis

Getting to an MRI is the bottleneck

Diagnosis requires only two things: a clinician who thinks of the condition and a cervical MRI that shows cord compression matching the clinical picture. The delay lives almost entirely in the first step. In a series of 42 patients who eventually had surgery, the mean time from first symptom to diagnosis was 2.2 years (SD 2.3), after a mean of 5.2 physician visits; 69% had first consulted a family physician, 43% had been diagnosed initially with carpal tunnel syndrome, and family physicians made the correct diagnosis in under 5% of cases (Behrbalk et al., 2013).

A UK study reconstructed the pathway for 43 patients from records (Figure 3). Referral from primary care came at a mean of 6.4 months after symptom onset, the MRI at 12.5 months, neurosurgical review at 15.8 months, and surgery at 22.1 months; half were first referred to a specialty other than spinal surgery, most often neurology, and 59% deteriorated measurably between the first assessment and the surgical one (Hilton et al., 2019). An online survey of 778 patients found that a delay of 1 to 2 years was typical, that longer delays were associated with greater disability, dependence on others, and unemployment, and that Black or African American respondents faced longer delays (Pope et al., 2020b).

Who should be suspected

Anyone over 50 with progressive numbness or clumsiness in both hands, unsteadiness or falls, stiff legs, or new urinary urgency, especially in combination, needs a full neurological examination and, if there is any sign of cord involvement, a cervical MRI (Davies et al., 2018). Hand symptoms in both hands at once, or hand symptoms with any leg or bladder symptom, are not carpal tunnel syndrome until proven otherwise. Rapid onset, fever, weight loss, or recent surgery point to other causes and need urgent assessment (Tetreault et al., 2022).

Reading the MRI

MRI shows the discs, ligaments, and cord directly and is the essential test; X-rays and CT show bone but not the cord (Davies et al., 2018). The radiologist reports where and how much the cord is compressed, how many levels are involved, and whether the cord's own signal has changed. A bright spot on T2-weighted images, T2 hyperintensity, indicates excess water from swelling or tissue loss; it is common but nonspecific and may or may not be reversible. A dark area on T1-weighted images, T1 hypointensity, indicates established tissue loss, or myelomalacia (Tetreault et al., 2022). A systematic review of prognostic imaging found that the number of T2-bright segments, combined T1 and T2 change, and the ratio of signal intensities predicted a poorer surgical outcome, whereas the T2 grade alone, the compression ratio, and the canal diameter did not (Tetreault et al., 2013). Because a quarter of healthy adults have compression, a compressed cord on MRI confirms the diagnosis only when it fits the symptoms and signs (Tetreault et al., 2022).

Electrophysiology and other tests

Electrical tests are not needed for diagnosis but earn their place in three situations (Tetreault et al., 2022). Electromyography and nerve conduction studies distinguish cord disease from carpal tunnel syndrome, peripheral neuropathy, and motor neuron disease; nerve conduction is typically normal in DCM. Somatosensory evoked potentials and motor evoked potentials measure conduction through the cord itself and help when the scan and the examination disagree, or when someone with silent compression is being watched for progression. Blood tests for vitamin B12, copper, and inflammatory markers are routine because they exclude treatable mimics.

The absence of agreed diagnostic criteria compounds the problem. There is no validated definition of what combination of symptoms, signs, and imaging constitutes DCM, and developing one is a stated research priority (Hilton et al., 2022).

Treatment

Surgery is the only treatment shown to stop the decline

Treatment decisions follow severity (Figure 4). The 2017 guideline from AO Spine and the Cervical Spine Research Society, built on five systematic reviews and graded with the GRADE system, makes four recommendations (Fehlings et al., 2017b). For severe and moderate disease (mJOA 14 or below) it recommends surgery, a strong recommendation on moderate-quality evidence. For mild disease (15 to 17) it suggests offering either surgery or a supervised trial of structured rehabilitation, recommends surgery if the patient deteriorates, and suggests it if they fail to improve; this is a weak recommendation on very low to low-quality evidence. For people with cord compression but no myelopathy it suggests against prophylactic surgery, with counseling about the risk and education about warning symptoms, unless there is also radiculopathy, in which case surgery or close follow-up are both options because the risk of progression is about threefold.

The mild-disease question

Mild disease is where the evidence is thinnest and practice most varied. The one randomized trial found no average difference between surgery and conservative care at 3 and at 10 years in 64 patients with mild or moderate, slowly progressive disease (Kadaňka et al., 2011). Against that, 193 patients with mild disease in the AO Spine cohorts had quality-of-life scores more than two standard deviations below population norms before surgery and improved significantly on every measure 2 years afterward, though the mJOA gain (0.87 points) was below the minimum clinically important difference while disability and quality-of-life gains were substantial (Badhiwala et al., 2019). A 2025 AO Spine appraisal of recent literature issued a conditional recommendation that surgery is a valid option for mild disease (Fehlings et al., 2025). Meanwhile a survey of 699 spine professionals worldwide found that most offer nonoperative patients standardized follow-up, most often at 6-month intervals, but that what the follow-up consists of, and what would tip them toward surgery, varied widely (Brannigan et al., 2024).

What nonoperative care can and cannot do

No nonoperative treatment has been shown to reverse cord injury or to change the natural history (Rhee et al., 2013). Structured rehabilitation for mild disease means supervised physiotherapy for strength and balance, activity advice, sometimes a soft collar, and scheduled reassessment to catch deterioration. Two things are to be avoided: cervical traction and spinal manipulation, which can injure a compressed cord and are considered contraindicated (Davies et al., 2018; McCormick et al., 2020). Pain and spasticity are treated on their own merits with neuropathic analgesics and antispasticity drugs, before and after surgery (Davies et al., 2018).

Operating from the front or the back

Surgery makes room for the cord. From the front of the neck, anterior cervical discectomy and fusion removes the offending disc and spurs at one or two levels and fuses the vertebrae; a corpectomy removes a vertebral body when compression extends behind it. From the back, laminectomy removes the bony roof of the canal, usually with screws and rods to prevent the neck from later slumping, while laminoplasty hinges the roof open and props it, preserving motion. The choice turns on where the compression comes from, how many levels are involved, and the alignment of the neck: anterior approaches suit focal compression from the front, OPLL, and a kyphotic neck; posterior approaches suit compression at three or more levels or from behind (McCormick et al., 2020; Badhiwala et al., 2020).

The only randomized comparison, 163 patients with two or more levels of compression, found no difference in patient-reported physical function at 1 year between ventral and dorsal surgery (gains of 5.9 versus 6.2 points on the SF-36 physical score), but complications were twice as frequent after ventral surgery (48% versus 24%), almost entirely temporary dysphagia (41% versus none), while dorsal surgery carried more new neurological deficits (9% versus 2%) and readmissions (Ghogawala et al., 2021). In a nonrandomized comparison within the same trial, laminoplasty had the fewest complications (10.7%). The 2025 AO Spine appraisal concluded that both approaches afford similar benefit with different complication profiles (Fehlings et al., 2025).

What surgery achieves

In the international AO Spine cohort of 479 patients, mean mJOA improved from 12.5 before surgery to 14.9 at 2 years, neck-related disability fell by a third, and physical and mental quality-of-life scores rose by about 6.5 points each; neurological complications occurred in 3.1% (Fehlings et al., 2015). The gain scales with severity, as the pooled analysis below shows: the sickest patients gain the most points but also start furthest from normal. Timing matters. In the AO Spine cohorts, patients operated on within 4 months of symptom onset had significantly better mJOA outcomes than those operated on later, although the mean duration of symptoms before surgery was 26 months (Tetreault et al., 2019). Most recovery occurs within 6 to 12 months of surgery, and deficits that remain after that are likely permanent (Davies et al., 2018).

Overall complication rates in the North American cohort were 11% after anterior-only and 19% after posterior-only surgery, and older age, longer operations, and combined front-and-back procedures raised the risk (Fehlings et al., 2012). The guideline's cost-effectiveness review, based on a Canadian cohort followed for 2 years and modeled over a lifetime, found surgery cost about CAD 11,500 per quality-adjusted life year gained, or about CAD 20,500 under the conservative assumption that unoperated patients would never decline; both are well within conventional willingness-to-pay thresholds (Witiw et al., 2017; Fehlings et al., 2017b).

Prognosis

Recovery is real but partial, and time-limited

Most patients improve after surgery and nearly all stop declining, but few return to normal. Alongside the average gains above, surgical cohort data cited by Karadimas et al. (2015) put the share of patients still substantially impaired 6 months after decompression at 44%, with 9.3% having declined. The research community's own summary is that surgery offers most patients meaningful benefit, that few make a full recovery, and that the condition carries among the worst quality-of-life scores of any chronic disease, with high levels of dependence and unemployment (Davies et al., 2022). Residual problems commonly include falls, reduced mobility, incontinence, pain, depression, and poor sleep (Davies et al., 2018).

The predictors of a poorer outcome are the ones this briefing has already met: more severe disease at presentation, longer duration of symptoms, and MRI evidence of established tissue loss, particularly T1 change combined with T2 change (Tetreault et al., 2019; Tetreault et al., 2013). Because these are largely consequences of delay, prognosis is to a large extent a function of how quickly the diagnosis was made. The survey evidence that longer delays go with greater disability, dependence, and unemployment makes the same point from the patient's side (Pope et al., 2020b).

Living with the condition after surgery means managing what remains. Neuropathic pain and spasticity are treated with medication, balance and hand function with rehabilitation, and the neck itself with sensible activity; the value of structured rehabilitation after surgery is one of the ten research priorities precisely because it has been so little studied (Davies et al., 2022). The patient charity Myelopathy.org, a registered charity in England and Wales, runs the largest international community for people with DCM and their carers and co-leads the research program described below.

Debates and outlook

Open questions, live controversies, and what is coming

The field organized its uncertainties in 2019 through AO Spine RECODE-DCM, a partnership of surgeons, other clinicians, and people living with the condition from 68 countries, which used a James Lind Alliance priority setting partnership to rank the ten research questions that matter most (Davies et al., 2022). The list is a fair map of where the evidence is missing: awareness, natural history, diagnostic criteria, assessment tools, the biological basis of injury, perioperative rehabilitation, novel therapies, socioeconomic impact, dynamic imaging and neurophysiology, and individualizing surgery. The same initiative produced the unifying name and definition and, in 2024, a core outcome set and minimum data set for future trials (Davies et al., 2024a; Davies et al., 2024b).

Whom to operate on, and when. The mild-disease question described above is the central controversy in practice. The 2017 guideline's weak recommendation, the null randomized trial, the favorable cohort data, the 2025 conditional recommendation for surgery, and the wide variation in what surgeons actually do are all consistent with genuine equipoise (Fehlings et al., 2017b; Kadaňka et al., 2011; Badhiwala et al., 2019; Fehlings et al., 2025; Brannigan et al., 2024). Trials that randomize mild disease with better outcome measures are the obvious next step, and identifying which mild patients are on a downward trajectory is a stated priority.

Measuring what matters. The mJOA is the field's common currency and a poor one: its ceiling hides change in mild disease, its steps are not equal, and it ignores pain, which patients rank highly (Tetreault et al., 2015; Kopjar et al., 2015). The riluzole trial's authors concluded that the scale was too insensitive to detect a drug effect (Fehlings et al., 2021). The 2024 core outcome set is the field's answer, and a 2025 AO Spine review argues for multidimensional assessment including pain, quality of life, frailty, and imaging (Davies et al., 2024b; Fehlings et al., 2025).

Neuroprotection. Riluzole, which protected the cord in animal models, was tested in 290 patients undergoing decompression in the CSM-Protect trial: the change in mJOA at 6 months was 2.45 points with riluzole and 2.83 with placebo (difference −0.38, 95% CI −0.90 to 0.13), a null result on the primary outcome, with a signal of less neck pain in secondary analyses (Fehlings et al., 2021). The anti-inflammatory drug ibudilast is being tested as an add-on to surgery in the UK RECEDE-Myelopathy trial, with pain and mJOA at 6 months as co-primary endpoints (Davies et al., 2023); when this briefing was compiled the trial registry still listed it as recruiting and no results had been published.

Seeing injury before it is felt. Quantitative MRI measures cord area, diffusion along fibers, and myelin content directly. In a longitudinal study of 26 nonoperative patients, such measures flagged progression in 68% over about 13 months, against 11.5% by mJOA and 42% by the patients' own impression, suggesting that behavioral adaptation masks ongoing tissue injury (Martin et al., 2018). The 2025 AO Spine appraisal conditionally recommends diffusion-weighted signal change for judging prognosis after surgery (Fehlings et al., 2025). Whether these tools can decide who with silent compression should be operated on is the practical question.

Counting the undiagnosed. The twelvefold gap between modeled and recorded prevalence, concentrated in the elderly, implies a large population living with a treatable cause of falls, incontinence, and dependence that is being attributed to age (Grodzinski et al., 2023). Diagnostic criteria, screening tools for primary care, and a dedicated diagnostic code are the prerequisites for closing it (Hilton et al., 2022; Nouri et al., 2015), and the evidence that delays fall unequally on Black patients makes this a matter of equity as well as efficiency (Pope et al., 2020b).
